# Low-Cost IoT + ML Cattle Behaviour Monitoring for Côte d'Ivoire: A Systematic Evidence Review (2020–2026)

**Bottom line:** I found no peer-reviewed accelerometer study on N'Dama, Baoulé or West African zebu cattle, and no public labelled accelerometer dataset from *Bos indicus* or African cattle. Your project would therefore be the first of its kind in that setting, and you cannot validate it across domains on target-breed data until you collect your own. For now, the best you can do is stress-test the model on several public neck-collar datasets sampled at 10 Hz or faster, from Norway, Chile and the USA. The latest evidence also says your current balanced accuracy of 0.95 under leave-one-animal-out (LOAO) probably overstates how well the model will work in the field. A June 2026 cross-year study saw macro-F1 drop from 0.94 to 0.49 when the model was tested on a new year and new animals.\[1\]

## TL;DR
- **Evidence gap:** African cattle-sensor research is almost all GPS mobility work: Sahel transhumance in Senegal, Maasai herds in Kenya, northern Tanzania, Sanga cattle in Namibia, Nguni/Bonsmara in South Africa. I found no accelerometer behaviour classifier validated on West African breeds, and no public *Bos indicus* accelerometer dataset. Collecting even a small labelled dataset in Côte d'Ivoire is the most valuable scientific contribution you can make.
- **Generalization is the main risk:** James et al. 2026 (Frontiers in Animal Science, five datasets) used leave-one-individual-out only on two datasets, and its cross-dataset transfer test was "exploratory". Uka et al. 2026 (arXiv:2606.24986) showed performance falling at each stricter step: random split, then grouped split, then LOAO, then a cross-year test. Report LOAO as your floor, add cross-dataset tests, and later add cross-season tests.
- **Fix three engineering issues before collecting field data:**
  - Mismatch between training and on-device preprocessing: you train on 100 ms bin averages but the device takes point samples at 10 Hz without anti-aliasing. Fix this by averaging on the device or enabling the IMU's digital low-pass filter.
  - Undefined collar orientation: standardise how the collar is fitted, and add features that do not depend on orientation.
  - GPS gaps: these are not random, because they depend on posture and canopy. Report fix success rate and never treat missing fixes as random.

## 1. Sensor studies on African cattle

### 1.1 Key findings
- **GPS dominates in Africa, and accelerometers are almost absent.** Every African study I could verify uses GPS collars to describe mobility or grazing. The one exception is an older Zambian study with 2D motion sensors. None trains and validates an accelerometer behaviour classifier on African breeds.
- **West Africa is covered only by Senegal's Sahel GPS work.** That work shows nocturnal grazing, step-by-step transhumance phases and night paddocking in fields.\[2\] All three bear directly on your daily activity baseline, because "normal" activity in agropastoral herds changes with season and herding regime.
- **No studies on Côte d'Ivoire, N'Dama or Baoulé turned up** in my searches. Treat this as a real gap in the literature, not just a weakness of the search, but confirm it with a database search (CAB Abstracts, AJOL) before you write the thesis.

### 1.3 Adoption and feasibility of livestock IoT in Sub-Saharan Africa
I found no peer-reviewed LoRaWAN livestock deployment in Sub-Saharan Africa with measured outcomes for cost, coverage or farmer acceptance. The Nigerian paper is a concept only.\[15\] Most LoRaWAN field evidence comes from European alpine pastures; one example is a *Sensors* 2024 path-loss modelling study in the north-western Italian Alps (doi:10.3390/s24102991).\[17\] Range and battery figures from vendors, such as "up to 15 km per gateway" or "10+ year battery", are marketing claims and not evidence.\[18\]\[19\] One relevant point for Côte d'Ivoire's extensive systems: an independent rangeland-connectivity write-up from Open Range Systems reports about 31% GPS fix success for a solar LoRaWAN ear tag on live animals.\[20\] This is a secondary, non-peer-reviewed source, so treat it as a warning to test, not as a benchmark.

## 2. Public cattle accelerometer datasets

### 2.1 Key findings
- **I found no public labelled accelerometer dataset for *Bos indicus*, tropical or African cattle.** The closest tropical work is the Brazilian Nellore study with the commercial Ovi-bovi sensor (*Sci. Rep.* 2022, doi:10.1038/s41598-022-06650-5; 22 animals; random forest accuracy 0.821, kappa 0.704)\[21\]\[22\] and a Brahman-cross steer study (*Sensors* 2024, 24:3171, doi:10.3390/s24103171; n = 10; 40 Hz collar with video).\[23\] Neither one has released its raw data publicly, as far as I could verify.
- **Four open datasets use neck collars at ≥10 Hz and include animal IDs:** Ito et al. (Japanese Black), Versluijs et al. (Nofence, Norway), Morales-Vargas et al. (Chile) and MmCows (USA). ActBeCalf (calves, 25 Hz) adds a fifth if you accept the age mismatch.
- **Several datasets "known" in the literature are less usable than they appear.** In James et al. 2026, three of the five datasets run at 1 Hz, Andersen 2024 does not report how many animals it contains, and James & Rial 2024 has one cow.\[24\] The CSIRO datasets (Arablouei et al.) are well described, but I could not confirm that they are publicly available.

## 3. Generalization across domains and breeds

### 3.1 James et al. 2026: how they actually validated
**Citation:** James H., Rial C., Giordano J., Erickson D. (2026). "Advancing standardized cattle behavior classification with a random forest model evaluated across diverse datasets." *Frontiers in Animal Science* 7:1676504. doi:10.3389/fanim.2026.1676504 (published 30 January 2026; CC BY; code at github.com/HannahJames123/cattle-behavior).\[24\]

- **Datasets:** the five described above (Feng, Ito, Mladenova, Andersen, James & Rial). They span nose-ring, neck, collar and ear-tag placements, rates from 1 to 25 Hz, and Holstein, Japanese Black, Simmental, mixed-breed and dairy cattle.\[24\]\[30\]
- **Pipeline:** one standardised feature-extraction pipeline built on accelerometer features and applied to all five datasets. Model selection used LazyPredict with default hyperparameters, imputation, scaling and **SMOTE** class balancing, and Random Forest came out on top.\[24\]
- **Validation:** **leave-one-individual-out (LOIO) only for Datasets 1 and 2**, "where individual-level metadata enabled testing generalization to unseen subjects".\[24\] The other datasets used within-dataset splits or cross-validation.
- **Cross-dataset transfer:** described as "an exploratory analysis to assess robustness rather than as a primary performance criterion".\[24\] The main case was training on Dataset 5 (ear tag, 1 Hz, barn) and testing on Dataset 2 (neck, 25 Hz), or the reverse, without retraining.\[24\] The authors state that "generalization across more heterogeneous datasets was more variable".\[24\]
- **What this means for you:** the paper supports your choice of Random Forest and on-device features. It does **not** show that a single model transfers across breeds or devices; it shows that a single *pipeline* can be retrained per dataset. Notably, the work was funded by the Bezos Earth Fund for virtual fencing in low- and middle-income countries, which is close to your use case.\[24\]

### 3.2 Uka et al. 2026 (arXiv, June 2026): cross-year evaluation
**Citation:** Uka L., Pinto S., Hoffmann G., Höhne M.M.-C. (2026). "When Multi-Sensor Fusion Fails to Generalize: Cattle Posture Classification Under Animal-Level and Temporal Distribution Shift." arXiv:2606.24986 (submitted 23 June 2026; preprint, not peer reviewed).\[1\]

- **Setting:** a pasture-based beef herd in Brandenburg, Germany. Lying vs standing was labelled by direct observation at 1-minute resolution.\[1\]
  - 2024: 106 animals, 9 days, 24,231 labelled minutes.\[1\]
  - 2025: 95 animals, 13,949 minutes, 43 of them cows not seen in 2024.\[1\]
  - Inputs were eShepherd collar tick counts, smaXtec rumen boluses and weather data. The classes were imbalanced (about 80% standing).\[1\]
- **Protocols, from least to most strict:** random observation-level split, then grouped animal-level split, then **LOAO**, then **cross-year evaluation on an independent cohort recorded one year later**.\[1\] XGBoost was the main model, with logistic regression, Random Forest and LSTM as baselines, all performing comparably.\[1\]
- **Results:**
  - Multimodal models reached within-year macro-F1 = 0.94 but cross-year macro-F1 = 0.49.\[1\]
  - The **collar-only model generalized best across years (macro-F1 = 0.54)**.\[1\]
  - SHAP analysis showed persistent reliance on bolus and weather features, which the authors read as shortcut learning.\[1\]
- **Related prior result:** the paper cites earlier work in which F1 fell by more than 50 percentage points when evaluation switched from stratified splits to LOAO, with some classes dropping from 0.91 to 0.15.\[1\]
- **Limitation to note:** an independent machine review (Pith) pointed out that unmeasured confounds between years could partly explain the drop.\[32\]
- **What this means for you:** keep the alert model based on the accelerometer alone. Do not add weather or GPS context to the *classifier* until you can test it across seasons.

### 3.3 Methods for handling domain shift (evidence I could verify)
- **Harmonising labels:** collapse labels to a shared coarse set before training on several datasets. The CKSP cross-species study, for example, merged 5 cattle behaviours into grazing, active and inactive.\[33\] Your binary Active/Resting target is the most portable label set there is.
- **Cross-species and cross-dataset knowledge sharing:** CKSP (arXiv:2410.16644) trained jointly on horse (100 Hz), sheep (12.5 Hz) and Japanese Black cattle (25 Hz) data.\[33\]
- **Sampling-rate selection:** a 2026 arXiv preprint (arXiv:2604.00517) tackles optimal sampling-rate selection and class imbalance using goat, cattle (Japanese Black) and horse datasets.\[34\] It is relevant for justifying 10 Hz.
- **Deep transfer learning:** this has been shown for sheep (Kleanthous et al. 2022, cited in CKSP). For cattle there is only within-dataset evidence.
- **Farm as the unit of evaluation:** Riaz et al. 2025 (*PLoS ONE* 20(6):e0325927, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0325927), a lameness study, compared n-fold with farm-fold cross-validation and found that "the n-fold cross-validation approach exhibited better but overoptimistic performance than when predictions were evaluated on a by farm basis". Validating by farm is the right analogue to validating by breed or region.

## 4. Best practices for validation and reporting

### 4.1 Cross-validation design (ranked by how realistic it is)
1. **Random or stratified window-level splits:** these leak information between windows from the same animal and should not be reported as the main result (Uka et al. 2026).\[1\]
2. **LOAO / LOIO (leave-one-group-out):** the minimum acceptable design. Your current design meets this bar.
3. **Leave-one-day-out or leave-one-session-out within each animal:** captures day-to-day drift.
4. **Cross-dataset (train on dataset A, test on dataset B):** captures shifts in device, breed and housing.
5. **Cross-year or cross-season on a new cohort:** the most realistic test, and the one where reported performance falls the most (0.94 to 0.49 macro-F1 in Uka et al.).\[1\]

### 4.2 Metrics and class imbalance
- Report **balanced accuracy, macro-F1, per-class precision and recall, and confusion matrices for each held-out animal**, not just the mean. Uka et al. show "individual variability reveals hidden failure modes", and some animals never showed both postures.\[1\]
- Imbalance is the norm: about 80/20 standing/lying in Uka et al.;\[1\] ActBeCalf is dominated by lying;\[35\]\[36\] and in Versluijs et al. rare behaviours each made up less than 0.1% of the data.\[28\] If you use SMOTE (as James et al. did), apply it **only inside the training folds**.
- **Reporting framework:** Riaboff L., Shalloo L., Smeaton A.F., Couvreur S., Madouasse A., Keane M.T. (2022), "Predicting livestock behaviour using accelerometers: A systematic review of processing techniques for ruminant behaviour prediction from raw accelerometer data", *Comput. Electron. Agric.* 192:106610.\[37\]\[38\] This remains the main reference for documenting each processing step: sampling rate, windowing, features, algorithm and validation. Stygar et al. 2021 (*Front. Vet. Sci.* 8:634338, doi:10.3389/fvets.2021.634338) found that only 18 of 129 commercially available livestock sensor technologies (14%) had been externally validated. That is a strong argument for publishing your validation openly.

### 4.3 Evaluating health alerts (recommended framework)
I found no single published checklist specific to alerts from accelerometer anomaly detection in extensive systems. The framework below follows standard practice for evaluating diagnostic tests, adapted to your veterinary case records:
- **Unit of analysis:** the animal-day. An alert is a true positive if it falls within a pre-defined window before or around a **confirmed** veterinary case (for example, −5 to +1 days).
- **Metrics:**
  - Sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value (PPV).
  - **False alerts per 100 animal-days.**
  - **Lead time:** days between the first alert and the clinical diagnosis.
- **Use of case categories:** "provisional" cases count in a sensitivity analysis only. "Ruled out" cases are informative negatives. Report results separately by disease category.
- **Avoid incorporation bias:** vets should not see the alerts when assigning case status, or you should at least record whether the alert prompted the examination.
- **Benchmark dataset:** MmCows includes `health_records` alongside 14 days of 10 Hz neck IMU data.\[39\]\[40\] It is the only public dataset I found where you could pilot alert evaluation offline.

## 5. Preprocessing and on-device considerations

### 5.1 Downsampling, averaging and anti-aliasing
- **Your mismatch is real and fixable.** Averaging 25 Hz data into 100 ms bins acts as a boxcar low-pass filter followed by decimation. Taking point samples at 10 Hz on the MPU6886 without a matched low-pass filter lets energy above 5 Hz fold back (alias) into the 0–5 Hz band. That inflates the std/min/max features compared with the training data.
- **Two ways to fix it:**
  - (a) Sample at a higher rate on the device (for example 50 Hz) and average blocks of 5 samples, which reproduces the training transform.
  - (b) Set the MPU6886 digital low-pass filter (DLPF) bandwidth to about 5 Hz or below and then retrain on the Ito data, low-pass filtered and decimated in the same way.
  - Option (a) matches the training transform most closely. Option (b) is cheaper in power.
- **The literature supports smoothing:** Versluijs et al. compared running means of 1, 5 and 20 s, and **the 20 s running mean gave the best performance** (accuracy 0.998, precision 0.991, recall 0.989).\[28\] The Brahman-cross study (*Sensors* 2024, 24:3171) explicitly tuned the smoothing window size.\[23\] A CSIRO patent on pasture-intake monitoring describes 10 Hz collars, **15-s windows**, pitch/roll features and a 0.3 Hz high-pass FIR filter to separate dynamic from static acceleration.\[41\] Your 15-s window is consistent with this.
- **Range:** ±2 g (Ito) vs ±4 g (your device) has little effect on resting/active features but changes how values saturate during vigorous events. Clip at the training range, or document why you don't.

### 5.2 Orientation and collar placement
- **Versluijs et al. 2023** found that "models based only on orientation-independent features did not perform better than models based on orientation-dependent features, despite variation in how collars were attached (direction and tightness)".\[28\]
- **Morales-Vargas et al. 2025** found that **body-frame signals discriminated behaviour better than world-frame signals** (maximum macro-F1 0.9625 with SVM).\[29\]
- **Why this still matters for you:** both results come from training and testing on the *same* fitting protocol. When you transfer across datasets (Ito → your collar), the axes will almost certainly not line up.
- **What to do:**
  - Add orientation-independent features (vector magnitude, ODBA, VeDBA) alongside the per-axis features. Obermeyer et al. 2025 (*Front. Anim. Sci.*, doi:10.3389/fanim.2025.1517570) showed that ODBA from simple collar accelerometers tracks grazing activity.\[42\]\[43\]
  - Write a fitting SOP covering the side the logger sits on, axis directions, tightness (for example, two fingers of slack) and a counterweight.
  - Log a 30-s static calibration posture at fitting time.

### 5.3 On-device / TinyML and LoRaWAN summaries
- **Embedded classifiers are feasible:**
  - Pavlovic et al. (*Sensors* 2021, PMC8231214) pruned a CNN for neck-collar data to fit "low-cost, low-power computationally constrained micro-controllers".\[44\]
  - Arablouei et al. 2023 (*Comput. Electron. Agric.* 207:107707) and a related preprint on knowledge distillation and fixed-point quantization (arXiv:2209.04130) ran in-situ classification on embedded tags.
  - A Random Forest with 12 features is small enough to convert to C or MicroPython if-else code (for example with m2cgen or emlearn).
- **Sending summaries instead of features:** a 2026 study on cattle behaviour classification with low-power LoRaWAN accelerometer data (PMC13306293) describes collars that compress the raw tri-axial signal into a single "Motion Index" per reporting interval. LoRaWAN delivered **98.5% of packets** over the study, at the cost of losing per-axis and frequency-domain information.\[45\]
- **Payload design for EU868** (EU868 is the frequency plan regulated for Côte d'Ivoire; confirm with ARTCI):
  - Per 15-min interval: the active-window count out of 60, a resting count, the mean of the class probabilities, and a GPS fix plus a fix-quality flag. That is about 10–12 bytes, well inside duty-cycle limits.
  - Keep a ring buffer of the 12-feature vectors on the device for later retraining.
- **Battery evidence for ESP32-class collars is thin.** The ActBeCalf Axivity loggers lasted about 27 days at 25 Hz ±8 g with no radio.\[35\] Vendor claims of multi-year LoRaWAN battery life assume very sparse uplinks.\[46\] You will need to measure power use on your M5Stack directly.

## 6. GPS fix loss and missing data

### 6.1 Verification of the two references you asked about
- **"Braem et al. 2024": NOT FOUND.** Five targeted searches found no paper by an author named Braem on GPS fix loss, location error or livestock tracking. Treat the reference as **unverified and probably mis-attributed**, and do not cite it.
  - The likeliest confusion is with Zhao B., McDermott R.L., Erickson G.E., Xiong Y. (2024), "Technical note: assessing GPS sensor accuracy using real-time kinematic device for livestock tracking", *J. Anim. Sci.* 102:skae253, doi:10.1093/jas/skae253.\[47\]
  - Caveat: Crossref and Semantic Scholar rate-limited my queries, so run a final Crossref author query before you rule it out completely.
- **Polojärvi et al.: VERIFIED.** Polojärvi K., Colpaert A., Matengu K. (2012). "Reduction of location error in GPS collar tracking data of bovine cattle by using data screening." *Journal for Studies in Humanities and Social Sciences* 1(2):1–18. University of Namibia, held in the UNAM repository (no DOI found).\[9\]\[48\] It tracked Caprivi Sanga cattle with Televilt Tellus Basic collars in 2006–2007, and its screening criteria included removing fixes with DOP ≥10 and implausible elevations.\[10\] The companion chapter is listed in Section 1.2 (doi:10.1007/978-90-481-9920-4_12).

### 6.2 Evidence that missing fixes are not random
- **Collar tilt and posture:** D'Eon R.G. & Delparte D. (2005), *J. Appl. Ecol.* 42:383–388, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2664.2005.01010.x. "The biggest source of variation was attributed to collar position, which resulted in significantly lower performance at angles below 90° from vertical."\[49\]\[50\] Lying and head-down grazing tilt the antenna, so fixes are more likely to go missing during exactly the behaviours you classify. That makes the missingness **MNAR with respect to behaviour**.
- **Habitat:**
  - Frair J.L. et al. (2004), "Removing GPS collar bias in habitat selection studies", *J. Appl. Ecol.* 41:201–212, doi:10.1111/j.0021-8901.2004.00902.x: "GPS error is predictable and biases the coefficients of resource selection models".\[51\]
  - Frair J.L. et al. (2010), *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 365:2187–2200, doi:10.1098/rstb.2010.0084: the main review of habitat-biased locations and how to correct them.\[52\]
- **Wildlife analogues for posture and canopy effects:**
  - Collars placed to mimic bear bedding sites got 16% fewer fixes, and statistical weighting recovered only 6% of the lost data (Sager-Fradkin et al. 2007, doi:10.2193/2006-367).\[53\]
  - Deciduous, mixed and coniferous cover were predicted to cut fix rate by 14.5%, 25.6% and 47.4% respectively (Vance et al. 2017, doi:10.1002/wsb.806).\[54\]
- **Canopy affects error even when fixes succeed:** Lewis J.S. et al. (2007), *J. Appl. Ecol.* 44, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2664.2007.01286.x. Fix rate was 97.9–100% regardless of canopy, but the 95% CEP ranged from 14.3 m to 557.0 m.\[55\]
- **Cattle-specific studies:**
  - Agouridis C.T. et al. (2004), *Trans. ASAE* 47(4):1321–1329: tree cover was the main factor degrading collar performance.\[56\] Its DOI was not verified.
  - Ganskopp D.C. & Johnson D.D. (2007), *Rangeland Ecol. Manage.* 60(4):350–358, doi:10.2111/1551-5028(2007)60[350:GEISAA]2.0.CO;2: immobile collars generated "about 1.7 m ± 0.7 m SE of perceived travel per record" with differentially corrected data (3.9 m uncorrected). According to a USDA ARS summary, cattle travel was "overestimated by about 15 percent" unless distances recorded while the animals were resting were removed, and the filters classified 81–92% of resting intervals correctly.
- **Low-cost receivers:**
  - A low-cost i-gotU GT-600 logger on cattle was much more accurate at recording intervals under 15 s (*J. Appl. Anim. Welf. Sci.* 2026, 29(1):1–8, doi:10.1080/10888705.2024.2395866).\[57\]
  - Solar GPS ear tags had a mean horizontal error of 41 m vs 2 m for Vectronic collars in stationary tests, and 59.2 m between paired devices on live cows (ScienceDirect S2772375525002540, 2025).\[58\]
- **Evidence gap:** I found no cattle study that directly measured fix success while lying vs grazing. The posture argument rests on stationary tilt tests and on wildlife.

### 6.3 Recommended handling
- **Report fix success rate** (successful fixes divided by attempts) for each animal, each day and each behaviour state, along with the HDOP/PDOP distribution and the share of 2D vs 3D fixes.
- **Screen out bad fixes, but document what was removed:**
  - Use movement-based screening (Bjørneraas et al. 2010, *J. Wildl. Manage.*), DOP and elevation filters (Polojärvi et al. 2012), and remove fixes taken while the animal was stationary before computing distance travelled (Ganskopp & Johnson 2007).\[10\]\[59\]
  - Screening can itself introduce bias (*Wildlife Biology* 2017, doi:10.2981/wlb.00229).\[60\]
- **Model the missingness instead of ignoring it:** Nielson R.M. et al. (2009), *Ecology* 90:2956–2962, doi:10.1890/08-1562.1, notes that "ignoring failed fix attempts may affect estimates of model coefficients", and its method stays unbiased with up to 50% of locations missing.\[61\]
- **For your system:** never compute daily distance or home range from days with low fix success. Flag those animal-days as missing, not low-activity. Keep the anomaly detector on **accelerometer-derived** activity, which does not suffer from the same missingness.

## 7. Recommended practices for this project

### 7.1 Adopt now (prototype)
1. **Remove the preprocessing mismatch.** Either oversample at 50 Hz and average blocks of 5 samples on the device, or set the MPU6886 DLPF to about 5 Hz and re-derive the training data with the same filter. Document which you chose in the thesis methods.
2. **Write a collar-fitting SOP:** axis directions, logger side, counterweight, tightness, plus a photo and a 30-s static calibration at every fitting.
3. **Add orientation-independent features** (vector magnitude mean/std, VeDBA, ODBA) to the 12 per-axis features. They are cheap to compute with Welford updates.
4. **Clip to ±2 g** before computing features, so that device data match the Ito training range.
5. **Log a GPS quality flag** (fix/no fix, HDOP, satellite count) in every packet and compute fix success rate per animal-day.
6. **Anomaly-detector hygiene:**
   - Exclude baseline days with less than about 80% data coverage.
   - Keep the 5-percentage-point minimum change and the MAD → MeanAD fallback.
   - Log every alert with its date so it can later be matched against veterinary cases.

### 7.2 Model-improvement phase
1. **Report evaluation in tiers:** LOAO on Ito; then cross-dataset tests (train Ito → test Versluijs, Morales-Vargas, MmCows, and the reverse); then pooled leave-one-dataset-out. Report balanced accuracy, macro-F1 and per-animal confusion matrices.
2. **Harmonise labels to Active/Resting** with a published mapping table for each dataset, for example grazing/walking/feeding → Active and lying/ruminating/resting/standing-idle → Resting. Run a sensitivity analysis on where "standing ruminating" goes.
3. **Resample every dataset to your device pipeline:** 10 Hz via an anti-aliased filter, then 15-s windows, then the same features.
4. **Prefer the model trained on pooled datasets** over Ito alone if it does better in leave-one-dataset-out.
5. **Pilot alert evaluation on MmCows:** run your Iglewicz–Hoaglin detector on its health records to rehearse the metrics (sensitivity, PPV, false alerts per 100 animal-days, lead time) before you have field cases.

### 7.3 Field deployment in Côte d'Ivoire
1. **Collect a small target-domain labelled set before trusting the classifier.** For example, 6–10 animals across N'Dama, Baoulé and zebu, labelled by video or direct observation in both dry and rainy seasons. Use it as a held-out test set only; this is the cross-season test that Uka et al. show matters most.
2. **Expect breed and management effects on time budgets:** compare Nguni vs Boran/Bonsmara, nocturnal grazing in Sahelian herds, and seasonal "tracking" by Maasai herders.\[7\] Baselines should be specific to each animal (as yours are) and stratified by season and herding regime, for example night paddocking vs transhumance.
3. **LoRaWAN EU868:** run a gateway coverage survey before deployment and send 15-min behaviour summaries, not features. Measure packet delivery ratio and battery life directly; there is no peer-reviewed benchmark from Sub-Saharan Africa.
4. **Veterinary ground truth protocol:**
   - Keep vets blind to alerts wherever possible.
   - Define the alert–case matching window before you see the data.
   - Count only "confirmed" cases as positives in the main analysis, with "provisional" cases in a sensitivity analysis.
5. **Data release:** publishing the West African dataset with animal IDs on Zenodo under CC BY would fill the gap this review identifies.

### 7.4 The 3–5 most suitable public datasets for cross-domain validation

| Rank | Dataset | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Versluijs et al. 2023 (Nofence, Norway)** | Neck collar, **10 Hz** (same as your device), **free-ranging extensive system**, 38 animals of several beef breeds.\[28\] The closest match to semi-mobile grazing, and the largest animal count among raw-accelerometer neck datasets. |
| 2 | **Morales-Vargas et al. 2025 (Chile)** | **Low-cost custom IoT collar at 10 Hz** (MPU9250/BNO055, hardware close to M5Stack/MPU6886), open pasture, cow IDs, walking/grazing/resting labels that map cleanly to Active/Resting.\[29\] |
| 3 | **MmCows (USA)** | 10 Hz neck IMU, 16 cows, 14 days, plus **health records**. The only public dataset where you can test both the classifier and the anomaly-alert pipeline. Barn housing is a domain shift you can use as a stress test. |
| 4 | **Ito et al. (Japanese Black)** | Your current training source. Keep it as one domain in leave-one-dataset-out, and use its 25 Hz raw data to test the averaging vs point-sampling question directly. |
| 5 | **ActBeCalf (Ireland)** | 30 animals, 25 Hz, ±8 g neck collar, with a ready-made **active/inactive** benchmark.\[35\] Useful as an extra domain for checking that the binary task holds up, though calves differ from adult cattle. |

Use Uka et al. 2026 to replicate its *evaluation protocol*, not as training data, because it provides tick counts rather than raw acceleration.

## Caveats
- **Search limits:** this review used about 18 web searches and one sub-investigation, not a PRISMA-style database search. Before submitting the thesis, confirm that no N'Dama/Baoulé accelerometer studies exist using CAB Abstracts, Scopus and AJOL.
- **Unverified items, all flagged above:**
  - The Versluijs replication dataset has now been identified as DataverseNO doi:10.18710/ND4CLL; the DOI 10.18710/8TWPS8 belongs to a separate Nofence heart-rate/ODBA study and is not the classification dataset.
  - Whether the CSIRO datasets are public.
  - Licences for most datasets.
  - Device ranges for Nofence and MmCows.
  - The Agouridis 2004 DOI.
  - "Braem et al. 2024".
- **Preprints and secondary sources:** Uka et al. 2026 is a preprint. The 31% ear-tag fix rate comes from a commercial blog. Battery and range figures from LoRaWAN vendors are marketing claims.
- **The Nguni/Bonsmara 2025 study** reports uniformly small p-values across many comparisons. Use it for qualitative breed context only.
